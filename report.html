01 — Introduction

Why Reclassify Shakespeare?

Shakespeare's 37 plays have been sorted into the same three buckets for four centuries: comedies, tragedies, and histories. This taxonomy was practical for the stage — it told actors what costumes to prepare and audiences what emotional contract to expect. But it was never designed to answer the question a streaming platform faces every day: what should I recommend to this viewer, right now?

The problem is not that the old categories are wrong. It is that they are too coarse for the granularity that modern recommendation systems require. "Tragedy" covers both the slow domestic suffocation of Othello and the sprawling political collapse of Julius Caesar. "Comedy" bundles the sharp, dark irony of Measure for Measure together with the breezy pastoral warmth of As You Like It. A viewer who loved one would not necessarily enjoy the other — and an algorithm that treats them as equivalent is failing its users.

There is a deeper problem too. Genre labels are descriptive, not experiential. They describe what a work is — its structural and formal properties as defined by literary tradition — but they say almost nothing about what it feels like to watch it. The viewer's experience is shaped by two things above all else: how the emotional register moves over the course of the work, and how the characters are connected to each other. Neither of these is captured by genre.

Research Question: If we set aside inherited genre labels entirely and classify Shakespeare's 37 plays using only measurable emotional trajectory and character network structure, what patterns emerge — and do those patterns better serve streaming curation than comedy / tragedy / history?

This project answers that question empirically. We analyzed two dimensions that directly govern viewing experience: the shape of the emotional arc across acts, and the structural properties of the character relationship network. By combining these dimensions across all 37 plays, six distinct types emerged from the data — each grouping works that produce a recognizably similar emotional experience, regardless of their traditional genre classification.

The result is a curation system that organizes Shakespeare not by what scholars have said about these works, but by what viewers actually feel when they watch them.

02 — Methodology

How the Types Were Derived

The classification was built in four stages. Each stage involved deliberate analytical choices — described in detail below — that shaped what the data could and could not reveal.

Stage 1 — Measuring Emotional Flow

To quantify emotional trajectory, we used VADER (Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner) sentiment scoring applied to the dialogue of all 37 plays. Every speech unit received a compound sentiment score ranging from −1 (maximally negative) to +1 (maximally positive). These scores were then averaged at the scene level and smoothed across the full play using LOWESS regression (bandwidth 200) to reveal the underlying arc rather than scene-by-scene noise.

Why scene-level, not act-level or word-level?

Word-level scoring would capture moment-to-moment fluctuations too granular to be meaningful for a viewer's overall experience — a single emotional line does not define how a scene feels. Act-level averaging, by contrast, collapses too much variation: a long act with violent swings in both directions would produce a mid-range average that misrepresents the actual experience of watching it. The scene is the natural dramatic unit — it is the level at which Shakespeare constructs emotional contrast and transition — making it the appropriate granule for this analysis. Five act-level means were then extracted from the scene data to compute the directional slope (Act 5 mean − Act 1 mean) that anchors the typology.

Why VADER, and why LOWESS smoothing?

VADER was designed for short, informal texts, which gives it sensitivity to valence-bearing word choices without requiring syntactic parsing — appropriate for verse dialogue that does not follow modern prose grammar. Its lexicon was not calibrated for Early Modern English, which is a known limitation (discussed in the Reflection section), but it remains the most widely validated general-purpose tool available for this kind of large-scale textual sentiment analysis. LOWESS smoothing was applied with a bandwidth of 200 to remove scene-to-scene noise while preserving the macro-level shape of the arc. A tighter bandwidth would over-fit to local variation; a wider one would erase meaningful mid-play shifts such as the valley in Break & Mend plays or the peak in Too High to Fall plays.

Stage 2 — Measuring Character Relationship Structure

Character networks were constructed by treating shared scene appearances as edges, with edge weight proportional to the frequency of co-occurrence (weighted degree). The Leiden community detection algorithm was then applied to identify internally cohesive character groups within each play. Four network metrics were extracted for each play: Density, Q Modularity, number of Communities, and Average Edge Weight.

Why speech co-occurrence rather than direct address or stage directions?

Direct address captures only explicit verbal interaction and misses the relational significance of characters who share scenes — witness scenes, group confrontations, ensemble moments — without necessarily speaking to each other. Stage directions are incomplete and inconsistently recorded across historical editions. Speech co-occurrence within scenes is the most consistently available and structurally meaningful signal: if two characters appear in the same scene, they exist in the same relational space, and the frequency of that co-existence is a reliable proxy for the strength of their narrative relationship. This approach is consistent with established methods in computational literary studies.

Why the Leiden algorithm for community detection?

Community detection identifies which characters cluster together into internally dense, externally sparse groups — the structural equivalent of "factions" or "social worlds" within the play. We chose the Leiden algorithm over the more commonly used Louvain method because Leiden provides stronger guarantees of community connectivity: it does not produce internally disconnected communities, which Louvain can. For dramatic network analysis where a "community" should represent a coherent group of characters with meaningful shared presence, this property is essential. The number of communities produced by Leiden proved to be one of the most discriminating variables in separating the six types.

Stage 3 — Classification

Plays were first sorted by emotional slope and negative-entry status (whether any act mean crossed below zero). This produced a primary ordering from which the broad directional types — ascending, descending, flat — were identifiable. Network profiles were then applied to distinguish types with similar slope values but different relational structures (particularly Warm & Bitter vs. The Fall, and Words Over Action vs. Against the Odds). Each type was validated by checking that its members showed internal consistency across both dimensions before naming.

Stage 4 — Curation Framing

Each type was translated into a streaming audience profile and viewing context, grounded in the emotional and structural signature identified in stages 1–3. The profiles are intentionally practical — designed to map onto existing recommendation logic that streaming platforms already use to match viewers with content by mood, occasion, and intensity preference.

08 — Reflection & Conclusion

What the Data Reveals

The most striking result of this classification is how little traditional genre boundaries predict emotional experience. Several significant findings run directly against received wisdom — and it is precisely these surprises that demonstrate the value of a data-driven approach over inherited categorization.

Finding 01

Othello belongs with the comedies — structurally

Othello is universally taught as a tragedy, yet its network profile — high density (0.669), low modularity (0.175), heavy interaction weight (2.49) — is identical to the Warm & Bitter type dominated by comedies. The intimacy that makes those plays warm is the same intimacy that makes Othello catastrophic. The data reveals a structural irony that close reading has always sensed but never quantified: these works share an architecture of closeness, and it is closeness itself that determines whether the result is warmth or devastation.

Finding 02

The Henry VI trilogy is one emotional journey, not three plays

Part 1 and Part 3 belong to Against the Odds (rising arc); Part 2 belongs to Break & Mend (valley recovery). Traditional classification treats all three as "histories." The data reveals something more precise: the trilogy enacts a complete emotional arc — descent into crisis, partial recovery, hard-won ascent. Watching the three plays as a single unit produces the same shape as a single Valley-type play. The genre label flattens what is actually a macro-structure.

Finding 03

King Lear is not The Fall — it is Break & Mend

Perhaps the most counterintuitive placement: King Lear, Shakespeare's bleakest tragedy, sits alongside A Midsummer Night's Dream and Henry V. Its act-level curve bottoms in the middle acts and recovers slightly toward the end. The data captures something close reading confirms: Lear's final scenes carry a fragile, hard-won clarity that is entirely absent from the unrelenting descent of Macbeth or Timon of Athens. The difference matters for curation — a viewer prepared for catharsis is not the same viewer who wants only devastation.

Finding 04

The Plateau type is defined by language, not genre

Love's Labor's Lost (comedy), Richard II (history), and Henry VIII (history) share minimal emotional movement and the corpus's lowest average interaction weight. What unites them across genre boundaries is a primacy of rhetoric over event — plays where language is the action. The data-derived type aligns with what scholars have long observed in each work individually but genre classification never encoded as a coherent group.

Limitations

Three constraints should inform how this classification is applied. First, VADER was calibrated for modern English. Its scores for Early Modern verse are imperfect — it may misread archaic negation, irony, or sustained metaphor. LOWESS smoothing reduces noise but cannot correct systematic bias in the lexicon itself.

Second, speech co-occurrence captures shared scenic space, not the full complexity of relational dynamics — power, affect, conflict, and silence are not differentiated. Two characters arguing violently and two conversing warmly register identically if their frequency of co-occurrence is the same.

Third, this classification reflects the original English texts. Productions in translation, significant adaptation, or with major cuts may shift both the emotional trajectory and the network structure substantially. The typology is a property of the text, not of any particular performance.

Application to the Streaming Library

The six types map directly onto recommendation logics that streaming platforms already use. The Fall and Too High to Fall serve the prestige drama audience — viewers who gravitate toward long-form psychological tragedy. Warm & Bitter and Break & Mend serve ensemble and catharsis audiences. Against the Odds functions as motivational or recovery content. Words Over Action serves the literary audience who approaches Shakespeare as text rather than spectacle.

The methodology is also portable beyond Shakespeare. The same two-dimensional framework — emotional trajectory plus relational density — could be applied to any dramatic corpus with available text data. Genre labels are a product of institutional history. Emotional experience is what viewers actually navigate. This project proposes that the latter is a more reliable foundation for curation at scale.
