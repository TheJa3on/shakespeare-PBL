01 — Introduction

Why Reclassify Shakespeare?

Our team is part of the content curation team at a global streaming service. The platform wants a new way to organize its library so that viewers can discover content through reference points like "Hamlet-type" or "Twelfth Night-type." This report is the first step: analyzing Shakespeare's 37 plays, grouping them into data-supported types, naming each after a representative work, and mapping each to the viewers it suits.

Shakespeare's plays have been sorted into three buckets for four centuries: comedies, tragedies, and histories. That taxonomy was built for the stage, not for recommendation. It is too coarse for the question a streaming platform faces daily: what should I recommend to this viewer, right now? "Tragedy" covers both the domestic suffocation of Othello and the political collapse of Julius Caesar. "Comedy" bundles the dark irony of Measure for Measure with the breezy warmth of As You Like It.

Genre labels describe what a work is, not what it feels like to watch. The viewing experience is shaped by two things above all: how the emotional register moves across the work, and how the characters are connected. Neither is captured by genre. This project classifies the 37 plays using only those two measurable dimensions.

Research Question: If we set aside inherited genre labels entirely and classify Shakespeare's 37 plays using only measurable emotional trajectory and character network structure, what patterns emerge — and do those patterns serve streaming curation better than comedy / tragedy / history?

02 — Methodology

How the Types Were Derived

Stage 1 — Measuring Emotional Flow

We applied VADER sentiment scoring to the dialogue of all 37 plays. Every speech unit received a compound score from −1 to +1. Scores were averaged at the scene level and smoothed using LOWESS regression (bandwidth 200) to reveal the underlying arc.

Why scene-level (not word- or act-level)?

Word-level scoring captures fluctuations too granular to reflect a viewer's experience. Act-level averaging collapses too much variation — a long act with swings in both directions produces a misleading mid-range average. The scene is the natural dramatic unit at which Shakespeare builds emotional contrast.

Why VADER and LOWESS?

VADER reads valence-bearing word choices without requiring modern grammar — suitable for verse. LOWESS at bandwidth 200 removes scene-to-scene noise while preserving the macro arc. A tighter bandwidth over-fits to local noise; a wider one erases meaningful mid-play shifts such as the central valley in Henry V-type plays.

Stage 2 — Measuring Character Relationship Structure

Networks were built from speech co-occurrence: characters sharing a scene form an edge, weighted by co-occurrence frequency. The Leiden algorithm detected communities. Four metrics were recorded: Density, Q Modularity, Communities, and Average Edge Weight.

Why speech co-occurrence?

Direct address misses characters who share scenes without speaking directly; stage directions are inconsistent across editions. Co-occurrence within scenes is the most reliable structural signal of dramatic relationship.

Why Leiden over Louvain?

Leiden guarantees internally connected communities, which Louvain does not. For dramatic analysis, where a community should mean a coherent group of characters, this matters.

Stage 3 — How the Number of Types Was Determined

The six types were not chosen in advance. They emerged from the intersection of two axes — emotional arc shape and network structure — applied to all 37 plays. The first axis sorted plays by the geometric shape of their LOWESS-smoothed sentiment curve, producing five distinct forms: continuous descent, continuous rise, mid-play valley (down-then-up), mid-play arch (up-then-down), and flat plateau.

Within the descending-arc group, a critical sub-split appeared. Some plays cross into genuinely negative sentiment in their final acts (negative-entry status), while others descend only within positive territory and never become bleak. Because these two sub-groups produce fundamentally different viewing experiences — one bleak, one bittersweet — they were separated into two types. The presence or absence of negative entry, rather than density alone, is the decisive boundary between Macbeth-type and Much Ado-type plays.

This yields six types: two descending forms separated by negative entry (Macbeth-type, Much Ado-type), one rising (As You Like It-type), one valley (Henry V-type), one inverted-arch (Othello-type), and one flat plateau (Love's Labor's Lost-type). We considered whether fewer or more types would better capture the data; six was the configuration at which every type was defined by a coherent combination of emotional and network signatures while remaining small enough for practical curation use.

✦ Boundary case. Antony and Cleopatra is a boundary case. Its slope is technically positive (+0.02) and its network is unusually fragmented (8 communities), neither of which match this type's typical signature. We classified it here on a stricter emotional criterion: although the slope is slightly positive, the play's overall sentiment stays within the cooling positive-to-neutral band that defines Much Ado-type — it never rises in the sustained way that defines As You Like It-type, and never enters Macbeth-type darkness. The fragmented network reflects its sprawling geography (Rome vs. Egypt) rather than a fundamentally different emotional shape. Our classification rule in such cases: emotional range and arc shape take priority over network anomalies.

04 — Conclusion

Summary and Significance

This project set out to test whether Shakespeare's 37 plays could be classified for streaming curation using only two measurable dimensions — emotional trajectory and character network structure — without relying on the traditional comedy / tragedy / history labels. The answer is yes: six internally consistent types emerged from the data, each named after a representative work, each defined by a coherent combination of emotional arc and network signature, and each producing a distinct, recognizable viewing experience.

The methodological contribution is a classification framework that is reproducible, data-grounded, and decoupled from genre tradition. Any new work — Shakespearean or otherwise — can be scored on the same two dimensions and assigned to a type or flagged as a boundary case. The framework converts a curation problem (which usually relies on human-written tags) into a measurement problem (which can be applied at scale).

The substantive contribution is the demonstration that these data-driven types cut across traditional genre lines in ways that better predict how a work feels to watch.

Finding 01

Othello shares its structure with the comedies

Othello, a tragedy, has the dense, tightly-bound network of the Much Ado-type comedies. That shared closeness is precisely what makes its betrayal devastating — intimacy turned lethal.

Finding 02

The Henry VI trilogy splits across two types

Parts 1 and 3 sit in the rising As You Like It-type; Part 2 in the valley-shaped Henry V-type. Together they form a single descent-and-recovery shape that the "history" label flattens.

Finding 03

King Lear is recovery-shaped, not pure descent

The bleakest tragedy lands in the Henry V-type, not the Macbeth-type — its late scenes carry a fragile clarity that a genre label cannot register.

Finding 04

Language unites across genre

The flat Love's Labor's Lost-type groups a comedy and two histories that share rhetoric-over-plot — a connection genre classification would never make.

For the streaming platform, this translates directly into curation logic. A viewer who finishes one Macbeth-type work can be reliably recommended another, regardless of whether tradition calls it a tragedy or a history. The six types map cleanly onto familiar front-end categories — prestige tragedy, bittersweet ensemble, journey of renewal, crisis-and-catharsis, intimate betrayal, and rhetorical wit — and the same two-dimensional method can be extended to the platform's wider film and series library.

Limitations and Future Directions

1. Sentiment tool calibration. VADER was trained on modern English and may misread Early Modern verse — archaic negation, sustained irony, and metaphor can be scored incorrectly. A sentiment model fine-tuned on period texts would improve accuracy and may resolve some boundary cases.

2. Network signal is structural, not qualitative. Speech co-occurrence captures that two characters share a scene, not the nature of their interaction. Adding sentiment-weighted or directional edges would capture relationship quality, not just quantity.

3. Boundary cases need confidence scoring. Antony and Cleopatra, The Tempest, and King Lear each fit their type on one dimension while diverging on the other. A production system would benefit from confidence scores or multi-tag assignments rather than forcing every title into one box.

4. Text, not performance. The analysis reflects original English scripts. Translations, cuts, or reinterpretive stagings can shift both the emotional arc and the network, so the typology describes the text rather than any single production.

These limitations define the agenda for the next stage: refining the sentiment model, enriching the network signal, building a confidence-scoring layer, and validating against specific adaptations. The system presented here is a working foundation — small enough to deploy, principled enough to extend.
